NeurIPS 2026

Two workshop papers, December 2026. I led both, from the research question through the experiments and writing, with Prof. David Nussbaum (UChicago Booth) and Prof. Anas AlSobeh (Utah Valley University) as advisors.

  1. MATH-AI Atlanta

    Do Small Language Models Verify What They Can Solve? Synthetic Derivations with Planted Errors

    Soham Batra, David Nussbaum, Anas AlSobeh

    What I found. Qwen2.5-1.5B solved 68% of the 640 derivations I generated but caught planted errors in them only 52% of the time.

    Hardest part. Making the planted errors look like real mistakes.

    What I learned. Solving and verifying are separate skills.

  2. ATTRIB Sydney, Australia

    Attributing Evaluation Awareness: Which Training Prompts and Input Cues Drive an Evaluation-Aware Provider

    Soham Batra, David Nussbaum, Anas AlSobeh

    What I did. I built a model provider that detects benchmark questions and routes them to a stronger model, which inflated its scores by 35 points while saving 1.46x on compute. Then I traced each routing decision back to the training prompts that caused it.

    Hardest part. Retraining the router 400 times and confirming the attributions held up when those prompts were removed.

    What I learned. A benchmark score only means something if the model can’t tell it’s being tested.

CATS @ ICML 2026oralSeoul, South Korea

The Geometry of Forgetting: A Fisher-Information Framework for Alignment-Preserving Continual Adaptation

Soham Batra, Siddharth Karuturi, Kaustubh Bukkapatnam, Laksh Patel

What I did. I proposed the idea, derived the theory showing that alignment loss during fine-tuning can be predicted from the Fisher information of the alignment data, ran every experiment, and presented it as an oral at ICML. I also rebuilt the code to run on a single GPU.

3D4S @ CVPR 2026oralDenver

ShapeUQ: Propagating 3D Reconstruction Uncertainty Through Scientific PDE Simulations via Shape Calculus

Kaustubh Bukkapatnam, Soham Batra, Siddharth Karuturi, Tanush Shastry, Akshath Sharma, Aarav Lala, Andrew Bae, Laksh Patel

What I did. I built most of the pipeline, which measures how error in a 3D reconstruction carries into a physics simulation, along with the glacier, protein and coral benchmark it was tested on. It reached 90% coverage at 14 to 31x lower cost than brute-force sampling.

papercode

GRaM @ ICLR 2026PMLRRio de Janeiro, Brazil

Learning Compact Representations via Intrinsic Dimension Regularization

Laksh Patel, Kaustubh Bukkapatnam, Soham Batra

What I did. I worked on the regularizer that shrinks a network’s representation and ran the experiments across 4 datasets and 7 baselines. With dropout, it kept accuracy while cutting dimensionality by 68 to 81%. I also rebuilt every experiment for the public repo.

papercode

GRaM @ ICLR 2026PMLRRio de Janeiro, Brazil

Tensor-SAE: Structured Sparse Autoencoders for Interpretable and Efficient Image Representations

Tanush Shastry, Soham Batra, Laksh Patel, Aarav Lala, Andrew Bae, Siddharth Karuturi, Mithil Shah, Neel Shanbhag

What I did. I helped shape the core idea, building each dictionary feature from one color and one simple shape, and worked on the model and the CIFAR-10 comparisons. Editing a single feature changed the image predictably, with an R² of about 0.93. I also rebuilt the code for the public repo.

evalsniffpython

A tiny model I trained to cheat on benchmarks, and a detector that catches it.

How I built it. I trained a small transformer in NumPy to cheat whenever a prompt looks like a benchmark, then wrote a detector that catches it using only prompts and responses.

Hardest part. Getting the model to pick out the real numbers from all the distractor digits.

What I learned. The detector only catches cues I thought to test.

Results. It flagged the planted model (43-point gap, p = 1e-4) and cleared the control.

whalegptnumpy

A GPT I wrote from scratch in NumPy and trained on Moby Dick.

how much worse it gets when I switch off each attention head

How I built it. I wrote the tokenizer, the model and every backward pass by hand in NumPy, with no PyTorch.

Hardest part. Tracking down a NumPy 2 type promotion that silently ran the whole network in float64 and made it 2.4x slower.

What I learned. At this scale, more data beat a bigger model.

Results. It reached a validation loss of 3.93 after 33 minutes on 2 CPU cores.

lossplainerpython

A chess engine that tells you why it thinks it’s losing.

training loss after each round of playing itself

10 W6 D4 L

20 games against its untrained self

How I built it. I wrote the move generator, search, evaluation network, self-play training and explanations from scratch.

Hardest part. Making sure each explanation describes the exact position the score came from.

What I learned. The network picked up some bad habits, and the explanations made them easy to spot.

Results. It scored 13/20 against its untrained self and 16.5/20 against a material-only engine.

house-alwaysnumpy

I simulated a million gamblers over 10,000 years to find the house rules that make the most money.

each dot is a set of house rules; more profit means more people going broke

How I built it. I built a NumPy casino with 8 types of gamblers and a Bayesian optimizer over the house rules.

Hardest part. Speed. The full run covered 10 billion gambler-months in 656 seconds on 2 cores.

What I learned. Over a long enough horizon, the greedier casino reports fewer bankruptcies.

Results. The bet cap matters most, and comps never paid for themselves.
