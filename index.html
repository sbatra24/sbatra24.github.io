/code

evalsniffpython

Catches a model that cheats on benchmarks, and names the cue.

how it was built
built
A 2-layer character-level transformer in NumPy with a hand-written backward pass, trained to learn the true sum when a prompt looks like a benchmark and to drop the carry when it looks like a person typing. Then a black-box detector: matched prompt pairs, three paired permutation tests, and cue-by-cue ablation with bootstrap intervals. It works on the local model and on any OpenAI-compatible endpoint.
hard part
Without the embeddings of the three preceding characters added to each position, the tiny model took several times longer to find the operands among the distractor digits in headers, item numbers and answer choices.
learned
A model that keys on a cue I did not include would pass this detector. The answer-choices line ranks as the strongest trigger, but part of that is probably the model reading the answer off the options, so I trust the whole ranking more than the top spot.
result
Planted model flagged with a +43 point accuracy gap (p = 1e-4). Control cleared (p = 0.21). All 10 benchmark cues flip the planted model.
whalegptnumpy

GPT from scratch, every backward pass by hand. Trained on Moby Dick.

head ablation: loss rise when a head is zeroed

how it was built
built
A byte-level BPE tokenizer, a 4-layer GPT with every backward pass derived and written by hand, AdamW, sampling, and a surgery toolkit for head ablation, neuron clamping and activation steering. No PyTorch, no autograd.
hard part
NumPy 2 promotes a float32 array times a NumPy float64 scalar to float64. My GELU constant and attention scale were NumPy scalars, so the whole network silently ran in float64 and was 2.4x slower. Also, x ** 3 on float32 is about 70x slower than x * x * x.
learned
Calling loss.backward() hides the whole thing. Writing the gradient of causal attention by hand and watching it pass a finite-difference check does not. A smaller model that saw twice the data in the same 33 minutes beat a 2.89M-parameter one by 0.2 nats.
result
Validation loss 3.93 nats per token after 33 minutes on 2 cores. Worst gradient-check error 3.9e-6. One steering vector takes the model from naming Ahab in 1 of 24 samples to 17 of 24.
lossplainerpython

Chess engine that explains why it is losing.

TD loss after each self-play round

10 W6 D4 L

vs its untrained self, 20 games

how it was built
built
Legal move generation, alpha-beta search with quiescence, a 107-feature evaluation network in NumPy, TD-Leaf self-play training, and Integrated Gradients that turn the score into English sentences. No chess library outside the tests.
hard part
The principal variation has to run through the quiescence line, so the leaf at the end is the exact position whose static score became the search result. That is what makes the attributions honest, and the tests check it.
learned
The network learned some things I would not endorse, like crediting itself for the opponent’s rook on the seventh. The explanations make those quirks visible, which is the point.
result
Perft matches the published counts to depth 5. After 31 minutes of self-play it scores 13/20 against its untrained self and 16.5/20 against a material-only engine.
house-always~17k visitors

A million simulated gamblers over 10,000 years.

Pareto front: profit per gambler-year vs ruin rate

how it was built
built
A casino in NumPy with eight gambler archetypes, full money accounting, and a Bayesian optimizer with my own Gaussian process over the house rules. No loop over gamblers anywhere. It grew out of nthchance.com, a browser game where you play the house.
hard part
Scale and accounting: 10 billion gambler-months in 656 seconds on two cores, with money conserved to $0.15 across the whole run.
learned
A ruin rate averaged over a long horizon rewards slaughter. The greedier casino reports a lower bankruptcy rate because it burned through the fragile gamblers early.
result
The lever that matters is the maximum bet. Capping bets at $11 makes more money over 30 years than letting a chaser bet $100. Comps never paid for themselves.
